Automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems have achieved remarkable performance in high-resource languages such as English and Mandarin, driven largely by the availability of thousands of hours of transcribed speech and the advent of deep neural architectures (Author et al., 2016; Author et al., 2020). However, this progress has not been equitably distributed across the world's languages. The vast majority of the roughly seven thousand languages spoken globally, including many African languages, remain severely under-resourced in terms of annotated speech corpora, pronunciation dictionaries, and language modeling data (Author et al., 2019). Among these, the Bantu language family, which encompasses over five hundred languages spoken across sub-Saharan Africa, presents a particularly compelling case study due to its rich morphological structure, tonal characteristics, and the scarcity of digital resources despite being spoken by hundreds of millions of people (Author et al., 2018).

Recent efforts to address low-resource ASR have explored transfer learning, multilingual pretraining, and self-supervised representation learning as means of leveraging high-resource languages to bootstrap performance in low-resource settings (Author et al., 2021; Author et al., 2022). While these approaches have shown promise, they often require substantial computational resources or access to related high-resource languages that may not share sufficient phonological or morphological similarity with the target Bantu languages. Data augmentation offers a complementary and computationally lighter alternative, artificially expanding limited training data through techniques such as speed perturbation, spectral masking, and synthetic speech generation (Author et al., 2019; Author et al., 2020). Yet, existing augmentation strategies are largely designed for and validated on non-tonal, non-agglutinative languages, leaving open the question of their effectiveness for Bantu languages with complex noun-class systems and lexical tone.

In this paper, we propose a novel data-augmentation method tailored specifically to the phonological and morphological characteristics of three Bantu languages: Swahili, Kinyarwanda, and Shona. Our approach combines tone-preserving spectral transformations with morphologically informed text-to-speech synthesis to generate diverse, linguistically plausible training samples. We demonstrate that our method significantly improves ASR performance under extremely low-resource conditions, offering a scalable path toward more inclusive speech technology for underrepresented African languages.

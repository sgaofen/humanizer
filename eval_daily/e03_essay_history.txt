# The Internet Caused the Bigger Shift in Who Gets to Publish

Throughout history, control over the flow of information has shaped power itself. Two innovations—the printing press and the internet—each broke that control in dramatic ways. While the printing press deserves credit as the first great disruptor, the internet caused a far bigger shift in who gets to publish ideas, because it eliminated not just the cost of reproduction but the need for any gatekeeper at all.

When Johannes Gutenberg introduced the printing press in the 1440s, he transformed publishing from a laborious, monk-copied process into something faster and cheaper. This allowed ideas like Martin Luther's Ninety-Five Theses to spread across Europe in weeks rather than years, fueling the Reformation. However, the printing press did not democratize publishing completely. Presses were expensive machines that required capital, technical skill, and often government or church approval to operate. A farmer, a laborer, or a woman with no formal education still had almost no realistic path to becoming a published author. Publishing power simply shifted from religious scribes to a new class of merchants, printers, and licensed publishers—still a narrow gate, just a different one.

The internet, by contrast, removed the gate entirely. Today, anyone with a smartphone can publish a blog post, upload a video, or share an idea with millions of people in seconds, at essentially no cost. A teenager in a small town can build an audience larger than many newspapers ever had. Social media, YouTube, and self-publishing platforms mean that literacy and access to a device are now the only real barriers, and those barriers are far lower than owning a printing press ever was. Movements like the Arab Spring, MeToo, and countless grassroots campaigns exploded specifically because ordinary people—not institutions—controlled the narrative.

Critics might argue the printing press did something the internet cannot claim: it was first, and it proved that mass communication could exist outside centralized authority. That historical significance is real. But "first" is not the same as "biggest." The printing press expanded publishing to thousands; the internet expanded it to billions, instantly, globally, and without institutional permission.

For these reasons, the internet represents the more radical transformation. It did not just speed up publishing—it erased the barriers to it, making the entire world of ideas open to anyone willing to hit "post."

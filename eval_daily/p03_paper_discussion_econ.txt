# Discussion

The estimated 4.2% decline in local retail employment following the minimum wage increase to $15 provides evidence that binding wage floors can generate meaningful disemployment effects in the retail sector, a labor-intensive industry with historically thin margins and high exposure to low-wage workers. This finding aligns with standard competitive labor market models, in which a wage increase above the market-clearing rate reduces the quantity of labor demanded, though the magnitude observed here is more modest than some earlier predictions based on simple elasticity assumptions.

The attenuated effect in counties bordering lower-wage states is particularly informative. This pattern is consistent with a spatial equilibrium mechanism: firms located near state borders face heightened competitive pressure from cross-border establishments unaffected by the policy, which may constrain their ability to pass through labor cost increases via price adjustments, while workers and consumers retain the option to substitute toward neighboring jurisdictions. The smaller employment loss in these border counties suggests that firms there may be absorbing costs through reduced profit margins, productivity gains, or informal wage compression among higher-paid workers rather than through workforce reductions—an adjustment margin worth exploring in future work.

Several limitations warrant caution. First, retail employment aggregates heterogeneous establishment types (e.g., grocery, apparel, big-box retail), and the average effect may mask substantial variation by subsector or firm size. Second, the analysis cannot fully rule out spillover effects such as cross-border commuting or informal employment shifts that are not captured in official employment counts. Third, the study window may not capture longer-run adjustments, including automation investment or firm exit/entry dynamics, which could amplify or dampen the estimated effect over time.

From a policy standpoint, these results imply that minimum wage increases are not costless, but their labor market consequences are sensitive to local economic geography. Policymakers should consider phased implementation and regional wage differentials, particularly for jurisdictions bordering lower-wage areas, to mitigate adverse employment effects while still achieving distributional goals for low-wage workers.

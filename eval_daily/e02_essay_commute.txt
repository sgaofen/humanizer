For three years, my commute had a soundtrack. Twenty-two minutes from my apartment to the office, filled wall to wall with tech news, true crime, and men in their thirties discussing philosophy like they'd invented it. I told myself this was efficient. I was learning while walking, optimizing dead time, turning a mundane trip into self-improvement. I had a queue of forty-three unplayed episodes at one point, which I treated less like a backlog and more like a debt.

The change happened by accident. My AirPods died on a Tuesday morning in March, and I'd forgotten the charging case at home. I almost turned back for it. Instead, annoyed, I just walked.

The first five minutes were uncomfortable in a way I didn't expect. My hand kept drifting to my pocket. I noticed I was slightly bored, and boredom felt unfamiliar, almost illicit. But then I noticed the actual street. A guy setting up folding tables outside a diner. Two crows fighting over something on the curb. The specific gray of the sky, which I'd apparently not looked at in months. I heard my own footsteps, which sounded different on concrete versus the metal grates near the subway entrance.

By the time I reached the office, I'd had three unrelated thoughts about a work problem I'd been stuck on for a week. Not because I was trying to solve it. It just surfaced, the way things do when you stop crowding them out.

I went back to podcasts for a while after that, but something had shifted. I started leaving the AirPods home on purpose, maybe twice a week, then most days. I'm not against podcasts. I still listen to plenty. But I stopped needing every empty minute filled with someone else's voice. Walking in silence, it turns out, isn't empty at all. It's just quiet enough to hear myself think.
